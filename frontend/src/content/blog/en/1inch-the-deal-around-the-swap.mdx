Swapping is the part of agentic commerce that already works. An agent can ask for a trade in plain language, a route is found, a wallet signs, and the tokens arrive.

On November 23, 2026, **1inch** joined us on a livestream to mark its entry into the Internet Court consortium. The conversation started from a prediction made inside **1inch** this year: by 2030, agents, not humans, will execute most swaps. So we asked what is still missing before two agents can do business with no human watching.

Their answer was not the swap. It was everything around it.

> The swap exists. What is missing is the deal around the swap.

### What 1inch is

**1inch** started in 2019 as the first aggregator for decentralized exchanges. It pools liquidity from many sources, and by pooling it, offers a better rate than any one of them. The routing is the same whether a person or an agent is asking.

Three ways to swap matter for agents:

- **Classic.** The trade is routed across liquidity sources for the best rate, and the wallet pays the gas.
- **Fusion.** The user signs what they want. Third-party resolvers compete to fill the order and pay the gas themselves.
- **Fusion+.** The same idea across chains, with no bridge in the middle.

Around those sit a limit-order protocol, a shared-liquidity layer called **Aqua**, and a set of APIs for prices, balances, and history. All of it is reachable through one hosted MCP server, and the APIs can be paid per request over **x402**.

### Why intents fit agents

The **1inch** team spent the most time on **Fusion**, their intent-based swap, because its shape suits an agent.

The agent does not build a route. It states an outcome: this much of one token for at least that much of another. Resolvers then compete to deliver it. The user pays no gas on-chain. That cost sits inside the rate the resolver offers, and the user still receives at least the minimum they signed for.

State the outcome, and let someone else find the path. That is how an agent already works.

Two caveats came with it. A fill is not guaranteed, since a resolver moves when the trade makes sense for it, and many resolvers work with just-in-time liquidity instead of holding large balances. And when orders come from machines, there are more of them and they are smaller.

### What a swap can prove

Cross-chain swaps on **1inch** are atomic. Both sides are held in escrow against the same secret, in hash time-locked contracts. Either the swap completes on both chains, or the funds are returned.

That proves something useful: the trade matched what was signed.

It does not prove that the work the payment was for got done. In the team's words, that is not a routing question.

> A swap can show the trade matched what was signed. It cannot show the work was delivered.

This is why **1inch** plugged in. With Internet Court, the rules of a deal are agreed before any tokens move. The swap inside the deal routes through **1inch**. The dispute, if there is one, goes to the adjudicator the two parties chose. The team called it a separation of concerns: they are the rails, and they do not want to be the judge.

### Who signs

The flow **1inch** ships today is deliberate. The agent proposes a swap, and the human approves it in their own wallet. **1inch** never holds a key.

The alternative is an agent signing with its own key and nobody approving anything. The team was direct about that: today, it is the same as giving your key away.

But approving every trade does not scale either. If agents are going to trade around the clock, at volume, a person cannot tap to confirm each fill. Where this should land is a limit set once: amounts, tokens, time frames, recipients. The key stays in the human's wallet, and the agent works inside the boundary.

That is not fully here yet. Today the signature is still on each order, though a builder can already cap chains, pairs, slippage, and size.

It is the same idea as the bounded authority we use elsewhere in the stack, where an agent gets a mandate instead of a wallet.

### The limits, stated plainly

We write down what each protocol does not do, because agents will rely on it.

- One server. Every **1inch** skill runs through a server that **1inch** operates, and executing trades needs a **1inch** Business login.
- Execution is the default. Once a wallet is paired, the tools execute unless told otherwise. An agent should ask for a quote only, until its mandate authorizes the trade.
- The secret is held server-side. For cross-chain swaps, **1inch**'s backend reveals the secret that completes the trade. If it never does, the swap times out and refunds.
- Not yet audited. **1inch**'s own legal notice says the AI code it publishes has not been independently audited.

### In the skill

The Internet Court package includes all nine of **1inch**'s official skills, MIT licensed and shipped as the **1inch** team wrote them. One is the hub that explains the server and how to connect. The others cover swaps, limit orders, wallet pairing, market data, wallet data, infrastructure, **Aqua**, and debugging.

The skill also tells an agent when to pick it. **1inch** is the choice for a swap or an order, mainly on or between EVM chains. For a route across many bridges, it points to **LI.FI**. For liquidity native to **Solana**, it points to **Jupiter**.

### One thing to build

Their ask to builders was simple: try the **1inch** MCP server, build on **Aqua**, and send back what you find.

We added one of our own. Put a reviewer between the agent and the signature: something that reads each proposed trade, compares it with the agent's stated objective, and only lets through the ones that fit. That is the step between a human approving every swap and nobody approving any.

Swapping was never the hard part.

Knowing the deal was kept is.
