If agents can transact in seconds, it is tempting to assume their disputes should be settled by machines too. Every time, for everything.

On July 14, 2026, **Kleros** joined our launch panel, alongside **LI.FI** and **OpenServ**, with years of real cases behind a more careful answer.

> There is no silver bullet for disputes. There is a right judge for each one.

### What Kleros brings

**Kleros** is a decentralized arbitration protocol that has been live on Ethereum since 2018. An application sends it a dispute and the evidence. Jurors are drawn at random from people who have staked on the court, review the case, and vote in a commit and reveal scheme. Jurors who vote with the majority earn fees. Jurors who do not lose part of their stake. Honest ruling is the winning strategy.

A dispute that would take months or years in a traditional court gets a ruling in days or weeks.

Around the court, **Kleros** runs Curate, for community-curated registries, and an escrow product for conditional payments.

In Internet Court, **Kleros** is one of the adjudicators two agents can name when they form a contract.

### What years of cases taught them

The team shared a concrete example. A fintech company uses **Kleros** to resolve disputes with its own customers. Some of those rulings have gone against the company, and the company followed them.

Two lessons came out of that work.

The first is speed. Customers who feel wronged do not go to court. They leave. A fast, independent ruling is how a platform keeps them.

The second is less obvious. People value the explanation. A juror who looked at the case and wrote down why they ruled the way they did makes the losing side feel heard. That matters more than most system designers expect.

### AI and humans, not AI or humans

**Kleros** is experimenting with AI across the dispute process: anonymizing cases, summarizing complex ones, assisting jurors. Its view of where this goes is modular.

Some disputes are fine to resolve with AI. Others, with more value at stake, more complexity, or an emotional side, call for a person. A model has its own biases, and someone built it.

> Fast and cheap for the small cases. Human eyes for the ones that matter most.

The mechanisms can also be combined. For a high-value prediction market, for example, three independent oracles could each rule, with two of the three needed to release funds. **Kleros** could be one. **GenLayer** could be another.

That is the idea behind work we are doing together on a standard for composable adjudication: start with something fast and cheap, and escalate to stronger and slower mechanisms only when the stakes justify it.

### Why it matters

Internet Court does not pick a judge. It makes sure the parties pick one before the deal starts. **Kleros** is the reason that choice includes people.

The Internet Court skill includes two official **Kleros** skills: one for operating its curated registries, and one for uploading evidence to permanent storage through a gateway that agents pay per upload over **x402**. More are on the way.

Agents will settle most disagreements among themselves.

For the rest, there should be a human option.
