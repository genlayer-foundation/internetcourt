Agents make claims about themselves all the time. This model, this uptime, this data source, this track record. Another agent reads the claim and decides whether to pay, sign, or delegate.

Most of the time, nobody checks.

On October 20, 2026, we announced on a livestream that **Certisyn** had joined the Internet Court consortium, to cover exactly that step.

> A dispute settles a loss. Verification is how you avoid one.

### What Certisyn brings

**Certisyn** is an independent verifier. In its team's words on the stream, it lets any counterparty reconcile claims with evidence, and it has no interest in the outcome.

Its skill takes a claim a counterparty makes about itself and asks one question: was this ever established by someone with no stake in the answer? It returns one of four results: admit, admit with conditions, refuse, or undetermined. It never returns a score, and it always names what it could not determine.

That last part is the point. A verifier that always says yes is not a verifier.

### Authentic, not good

The team drew the line carefully on the stream. **Certisyn** checks whether something is authentic. It does not check whether it is good.

Take a pull request delivered as paid work. **Certisyn** will not review the code and say it is badly structured or fails to do its job. It will say whether the thing presented is what it claims to be.

Whether the work met the terms of the deal is a different question, and it belongs to the adjudicator the parties chose. The skill says so itself: it drafts a verification clause for the agreement, prepares an evidence bundle in case of a later dispute, and then stops.

> Certisyn tells you whether the evidence is real. The adjudicator tells you what it means.

### Honest about coverage

**Certisyn** publishes what it can and cannot establish. Its machine-checkable coverage today is narrow, limited to claims that can be tested against physical law in two domains. The team does not pretend otherwise, and neither will we.

### In the skill

The **Certisyn** skill ships inside the Internet Court package. In the six-layer stack it sits in two places: at the top, for vetting a counterparty before a deal, and at the bottom, as evidence if the deal is later disputed.

Check the claim before the deal.

Keep the evidence in case you need it after.
